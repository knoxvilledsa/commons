The Knoxville Free Press: January 2026

Saturday, January 10, 2026

Welcome to the first issue of The Knoxville Free Press, the monthly newsletter of the Knoxville Democratic Socialists of America (DSA). The goal of our newsletter is to share updates about issues that impact the lives of working people, both inside our local Knoxville community and farther afield. Our hope is to use these short updates to not only keep us informed on the variety of ways people around our county, our state, our nation, and our world are working for justice and equality, but also to help inspire you to join us in taking action.

If you’re interested in joining the work of the Knoxville DSA, please join us at our next monthly meeting (the fourth Monday of each month) or consider joining one of our working groups.

Local News

The State of Things in the Scruffy City

Jason Galvas

In 2025, Knoxville’s political landscape was defined by the adage that “the more things change…”, well, you can finish that phrase yourself. There were plenty of changes in leadership during 2025 and positioning for the 2026 county-wide elections. The city of Knoxville itself remains a Democratic leaning “blue-bubble” in a deeply Republican state. Because of this, our city and county systems continue to operate under a split political personality.

At the city level, led by Mayor Indya Kincannon, now in her second term, the agenda has focused on emergency measures for food assistance, primarily due to the government shutdown and the loss of benefits for many people in Knoxville. Additionally, major infrastructure projects, such as re-opening the long-suffering and long-closed Gay St. Bridge (at least to pedestrian and bike traffic), were completed.

Then there is the county level, led by Mayor Glenn Jacobs. The county government has remained, unsurprisingly, quiet amid our ongoing crises. Mayor Jacobs began his political career here in the Knoxville area as a staunch Libertarian. As the wind shifted toward the diaspora that we have now, he swung hard for the fences, or the more apt metaphor for Mayor Jacobs, jumped off the top rung of the ring, falling right in line with the rest of the MAGA cult. The feeling is that he has checked out and is moving on to bigger and worse things, all related to a Blackburn campaign for governor. Already in 2025, a few high-profile individuals like Larsen Jay and Kim Frasier have launched campaigns to succeed him.

The most impactful event of 2025 was the November municipal election, which led to a massive turnover on the Knoxville City Council. On December 20, 2025, five new members were sworn in: Karyn Adams, District 1; Nathan Honeycutt, District 2; Doug Lloyd, District 3; Matthew Debardelaben, District 4; and Denzel Grant, District 6 (more on each new member below!). A sales tax increase was included in the municipal election. This was rejected by the vast majority of voters who cast ballots in the election. After the dust settled, the rejection of the tax increase was attributed to public pushback against the rising cost of living despite the city’s growth.

At the top of the flashpoints that defined 2025 was housing and people experiencing homelessness. The city launched a three-year strategic plan for “Housing Stability” late in 2025. This includes a new warming center network and increased funding for affordable housing units in the Old City and Burlington areas. While admirable, this is, as we are all aware, a band-aid on an open wound that will not be fixed by warming centers or by more housing that will remain out of reach for most Knoxville residents.

Also of note was the University of Tennessee coming under national scrutiny around free speech and education. Massive faculty and student protests and marches ensued after the university quickly capitulated to the current administration and suspended Dr. Tamar Shirinian over her personal comments on her personal social media, concerning the death of Charlie Kirk. This has sparked a statewide and nationwide debate on First Amendment rights on campus. As of this writing, Dr. Shirinian has sued the University to be allowed back into the classroom, but the request was denied by U.S. District Judge Katherine Crytzer. This will undoubtedly spill into our 2026 as this issue winds its way through the court system.

And lest we forget! Mayor Indya Kincannon abruptly proposed selling 12.7 acres of public land in Chilhowee Park to a private religious institution. This city council was given only four days’ notice before they were required to vote on the proposal. The backlash from the community was swift and loud. After a few adjustments and much handwringing from all parties involved, the matter was dropped, and our public park remains in public hands. This shows the power we possess in our community, and one we need to continue to exercise as we move into 2026 and the new challenges that await us.

What to Look Forward to as the New City Council Takes Shape

Jason Galvas

As we begin 2026, a slate of new Knoxville City Council members will assume office with fresh ideas and agendas they hope to enact. While specific policy details will evolve as they begin their terms, we must remain vigilant and hold the core platforms and priorities of the newly elected members accountable. There is much to be excited about and a few things to watch out for as the year unfolds. Let’s take a quick refresher on what each new member’s platform was and what to look forward to in the coming year.

1st District, Karyn Adams (South Knoxville):

  • Affordable Housing and Homelessness: She aims to prioritize solutions to address the rising cost of living and the city’s unhoused population.
  • Environmental Preservation: A strong emphasis on protecting South Knoxville’s green spaces and urban wilderness.
  • Infrastructure: Based on resident feedback, Karyn Adams will be focusing on improving pedestrian safety and local road maintenance.

2nd District, Nathan Honeycutt (West Knoxville):

  • Sustainable Growth: He is seeking to balance the rapid, intense development along the Kingston Pike corridor with the character of existing West Knoxville neighborhoods.
  • Public Safety: Supports a “balanced approach” emphasizing both police resources and community-based safety initiatives.
  • Green Economy: Supporting energy efficiency, weatherization programs, and EV infrastructure.

3rd District, Doug Lloyd (Northwest Knoxville):

  • Cutting Red Tape: Specifically focused on streamlining the building permit process and making code enforcement more efficient for developers and homeowners. This appears to be at odds with Nathan Honeycutt’s sustainable growth plan.
  • Fiscal Responsibility: He is advocating for leaner (read none) city spending and “back-to-basics” government services. Whatever those basics are, we’ll see, but this is something to stay very vigilant about.

4th District, Matthew DeBardelaben (Northeast Knoxville):

  • Connectivity: He is promoting “protected bike lanes” and creative traffic solutions to fill gaps in district corridors.
  • Housing Variety: Encouraging middle housing (duplexes and townhomes) to increase density and affordability without disrupting the neighborhood feel.
  • Quality of Life: Investing in sidewalks near schools and enhancing community green spaces.

6th District, Denzel Grant (East Knoxville/Downtown):

  • Violence Reduction: He seeks to leverage his community outreach experience to address crime through social investment and prevention programs. We will see how this works with the more “balanced approach” that other council members support.
  • Equity and Inclusion: Focused on ensuring minority voices are at the table for major development projects. This will require all of our support and voices to ensure it remains a priority in the current political climate.
  • Accountability: Promoting transparency in how public money is spent on community partners and non-profits.

Whether you agree with or disagree with the council members’ priorities in whole or in part, we encourage everyone to attend city council meetings to make your voice heard and your concerns known. There is power in our numbers and our voices. I look forward to seeing you there.

State Perspectives

Reflecting on the Aftyn Behn Campaign

Siena Fulton

As we near the end of the first year of the second Trump administration, and midterms loom around the corner, it is good to reflect on the political comings and goings of the year. This year saw marked advances towards full executive control, placing more and more power in the hands of the White House, and even further sidelining Congress. For many Americans, the House of Representatives feels more like a useless appendage than a body able to enact legislation that Americans have been calling on for decades, including healthcare, tax reform, and budget reform.

The shutdown, the longest in U.S. history (a whopping 43 days), came frustratingly close to meeting its goal: extending health insurance subsidies for our people. This shutdown was detrimental to almost everybody in the nation, perhaps most of all (politically, that is) the Republican majorities in the House and Senate, who faced public relations backlash. However, in the end, the Senate Dems caved, leaving it all on the table, shorting the people once more and handing a victory to the White House.

As a nearly lifelong socialist, I have often found myself totally disillusioned by electoral politics. However, this fall, I was in the opposite position. I canvassed for Aftyn Behn, a member of the Tennessee House of Representatives who was nominated for a special election in Nashville’s District 7 against the sinister Republican henchman Matt van Epps. I drove to Nashville one cold, rainy November morning with some other Knoxvillians, all people who had been previously involved in Behn’s campaign. These folks were mostly much older than me, but once we all got together in Nashville, I found a few people who must have been in my age-range. I got the sense that this moment was unfolding regardless of generation, reflecting a broad scope of discontent for our current state of affairs and emphasizing the importance of a fresh campaign like Behn’s.

Photo: Zach Kinslow, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

This experience was totally new. I had never canvassed; I had never supported a Democrat politician outright; and I have scarcely been to Nashville. That morning, I ended up learning quite a bit about how the on-the-ground race for one of our highest national offices proceeds.

Immediately, I ended up with Behn’s parents for our canvassing mission. This was strange to me, but they were very kind. We talked at length about their daughter’s dedication to the race, her willingness to win, and the tough battle for votes in District 7. Seeing it all from this perspective made me feel warmly about our political process and our democracy, despite all its faults.

As we drove around Nashville, handing out fliers and knocking on doors, we met all kinds of people. We were primarily in a section of Nashville that is experiencing the perennial process of gentrification. Towering, million dollar, brand-new condos surrounded older, stout ranch houses and bungalows. We met families, bachelors, Black residents, and white residents; some were visibly affluent, others less so. Everybody was kind, honest, and well-meaning. This was due to our placement in a neighborhood that was already blue-leaning, but even if it did feel a bit like an illusion, it still made me very optimistic.

I took a lot of time to reflect on all of this, and tried to synthesize my distaste for electoral politics and this positively real action I was undertaking. I began to see a way in which progressive, socialist policies can be fought for on some level, at least, on the electoral battlefield.

Behn is a lifelong advocate for progressive policy, a native Knoxvillian, and a successful politician, all at a young age. Her campaign represents a millennial progressive class that I dearly hope will manifest more in national politics as the older generations depart from office. Even though each platform may not line up with what you or I personally believe, what is important is that the movement be grassroots and not aligned with the interests of industries. For me, this is what was crucial about Behn’s campaign. What is so important about grassroots campaigns like these that emphasize progress and deviate from the Democrat party’s main platform, is that they can help to stymie the encroaching advance of techno-fascist executive control that continues to harangue our nation. By focusing on issues like grocery taxes, hospital upkeep, and clean funding, Behn is resolutely a member of that class of Democrat politicians that does not accept aid from super-PACs and large, corporate donors. This platform separates her from the languishing Democrat political class and proves her stature as a progressive politician.

Aftyn Behn did not win in December, but she got awfully close. In a nationally observed election, Behn proved that people everywhere, even in the South, are getting tired of Republican cronies who serve only big corporations. People are realizing that the establishment Democrat party cannot possibly bear the responsibility of opposing the Republican machine. Elections like this, and actions like canvassing, are becoming integral components of our fight, as socialists, to take back the power and visibility that we need in the long run.

Electing politicians like Aftyn Behn or Zohran Mamdani represents a return of the left wing to power. This is a power that the American left has not had in decades, for arguably half a century. To advance the politics of socialism, liberation, justice, and growth that we know must be advanced, we must first do everything possible to seize political power and begin to tear down the huge scars of propaganda that divide the American left from the base of mass support it needs to win.

International Perspectives

No Nukes! The TPNW, Nuclear Weapons, and International Law

Joshua Raposa

On the eve of its inauguration into law, Jon Letman of Truthout commented on the ratification of the UN Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW):

When the TPNW enters into force on January 22, nuclear weapons will become illegal under international law, joining other controversial weapons that have been banned, such as biological weapons (in 1975), chemical weapons (1997), mines (1999) and cluster munitions (2010).

Certainly, the TPNW was a welcome development in the so-called civilized world. While the ruling on the general prohibition on the use of nuclear weapons is new, the question of (or dispute on) their legality is not.

Many progressives dispute the utility of the law, arguing that is merely an instrument of the elite and of no material value to the working class; however, while it is undoubtedly the case that the law is used and manipulated for political ends, and that there are serious limitations to inciting social change via the law, this does not mean it does not have an important weight as far as public opinion is concerned.

In the battle for the hearts and minds of the American public, we ought to use every non-violent means at our disposal: the American public is incredibly sympathetic to concepts of ‘law and order.’ As such, when the law is on our side, we would be best disposed to understand it; and in the case of nuclear weapons, American socialists, in particular, have an immense task set before us.

Letman notes that of the 13,400 nuclear weapons that these States possess, “90 percent belong to Russia and the U.S.” Therefore, at least “90 percent [of the responsibility] belong[s] to Russia and the U.S.”

With that having been said, the question of the legality of the use of nuclear weapons was posed to the International Court of Justice (ICJ) in late 1994; and while the ICJ would eventually rule on their legality under very narrow circumstances (namely, when the existence of the State was at hand), it could be argued that they were illegal under preexisting international humanitarian law (IHL).

For instance, the use of nuclear weapons is in explicit violation of the 1907 Hague Convention, the 1925 Geneva Protocol, as well as the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT).

Article 23(a) of Hague Convention states: “it is especially forbidden to […] employ poison or poisonous weapons.” The United States government, however, asserted in its 1994 written submission to the Court that the 1907 Hague Convention on the use of poison weapons does not debar the use of nuclear weapons for “[i]t was not intended to apply … to weapons that are designed to injure or cause destruction by other means, even though they also may create toxic byproducts.”

While it could be plausibly argued that nuclear weapons may not fall under the category of poison per se (that is to say, it is not the intended effect of the weapon itself), one cannot say this for the term “poisonous weapon.” Trivially, the term “poisonous” modifies “weapon” such that the notion of “toxic [radioactive] byproduct” falls within its scope. As such, nuclear weapons would be considered illegal as a result of the second clause in Article 23(a).

In addition, nuclear weapons are clearly banned under the 1925 Geneva Protocol: “Whereas the use in war of asphyxiating, poisonous, or other gases and of all analogous liquids, materials, or devices, has been condemned by the general opinion of the civilized world…” (emphasis added).

Though many of the world’s conflicts appear “contained,” this likelihood decreases as the world’s foremost military powers continue to bolster their nuclear arsenals and implement policies of “nuclear modernization” (i.e., creating smaller yield warheads). Conflicts in eastern Europe to the south China sea could easily, and at any moment, escalate to the point of no return.

For instance, Reuters reported that “U.S. President Donald Trump indicated he would allow the last U.S.-Russia strategic arms control treaty to expire without accepting an offer from Moscow to voluntarily extend its caps on deployments of the world’s most powerful nuclear weapons, according to remarks released on Thursday.”

“If it expires, it expires,” the President reportedly said.

As to whether we can avoid the risk of nuclear annihilation and divorce ourselves from these weapons will be up to us to decide. As socialists, we ought to be committed to a world system of international peace, free from the tyranny of extinction. As a result, we ought to embrace the ratification of the TPNW as a new tool in the battle for public opinion and hold our government accountable to its just terms.
