Local Perspectives

Flock Safety Cameras: Intrusive Surveillance and the Illusion of Safety

Pixl

There has been a huge increase in adoption of AI-powered digital surveillance tools by local police precincts, state governments, and federal agencies in the last few years. One such tool adopted through the United States, including in Knoxville and East Tennessee, are Automated License Plate Reader cameras (ALPR). Despite the name, these cameras capture far more information than license plates.

These cameras can be used to track every vehicle that passes their camera, but the cameras also activate whenever they detect motion. Even if no license plate is detected, that data is stored on the device and without a warrant. This technology allows police to remotely track data on the movements of anyone who simply passes in front of these cameras at any time for any reason.

In 2022 the city of Knoxville contracted with Flock Safety to install and integrate AI-powered Flock Safety Cameras ALPR into the Knoxville Police Department (KPD). For the next nearly three years these cameras were active under that contract, which constituted major privacy violations, even if only used as intended.

Photo: Tony Webster, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.

The available information shows that Knoxville had at least 18 of these cameras, and Knox County has at least 28 cameras that are still operational at time of writing.

In early 2025, the City of Knoxville chose to swap vendors for its AI-powered surveillance technology from Flock Safety to Axon Enterprise’s Fusus system in a $27.5 million, 5-year contract. The city council meeting in which this decision was made was highly contentious; two city council members, Seema Singh and Amelia Parker, voted against the measure on concerns of how these cameras would be used to police minority communities.

In late 2025, several cybersecurity researchers and journalists, including John “GainSec” Gaines and Benn Jordan discovered and publicized extremely concerning security vulnerabilities in Flock Safety’s cameras. According to Jordan, these vulnerabilities could grant a malicious actor enough control over the device to potentially live stream the video feed to their own private server and save the data, which could be used to track anyone whose vehicle passed in front of the sensor. A malicious actor could tamper with the data gathered by the device and, because Gaines also discovered full root access vulnerabilities to the device, it could be virtually impossible to guarantee that the data collected by the device was un-tampered with, which would call into question every case in which this data was used as evidence by police.

A cybersecurity researcher named Joshua Michael also discovered that websites intended as tech demonstrations for sale to police departments were so vulnerable as to be findable with a Google search and could be used to gain access to live camera feeds, track the location of police and other public safety responders.

According to East Tennessee privacy activists, Knoxville had used Axon Enterprises as a vendor for tasers and body cameras before switching their ALPR system contract from Flock in early 2025. This switch seemingly led Flock to cut the KPD’s access to the Flock safety camera network on January 1, 2026, despite efforts to negotiate an extension of the contract that would last until the Axon cameras were functional.

This whole messy business has revealed that the City of Knoxville employed extremely insecure AI-powered surveillance devices for almost three years. This potentially exposed any motorists who crossed paths with these sensors to privacy violations and put them at risk of stalking and harassment. Axon Enterprise was an early investor in Flock Safety before splitting to develop a new standalone business. What reason is there to trust that Axon will do a better job securing their cameras than Flock?

The City of Knoxville and the KPD have not demonstrated that they did any better security vetting of these new cameras than they did of the old ones. Jordan’s reporting shows a troubling degree of carelessness on behalf of the ALPR vendors. Extremely common security steps, such as Two-Factor Authentication would resolve several of these security vulnerabilities, but this requires vendors or the local governments to implement these simple solutions.

Additionally, this technology has already been used by police to stalk and harass people, including their romantic partners. According to the report, police officers can use vague terms such as “investigate,” “suspicious,” or even just “.” to access the network. With these questions about potential abuse of the technology looming, the Milwaukee Police Department reportedly cut access to their Flock camera system while reviewing the policy concerning use of the technology.

It is in the interest of all of us to contact the city and let them know the vulnerabilities they have exposed us to in their rush to employ the latest cutting edge surveillance technology. It is also in our best interest to stay aware of developments with this contract between Knoxville and Axon Enterprise and other security vulnerabilities that might be revealed.

The Life (and Death) of McKay’s in Knoxville

Jason Galvas

Part 1: A Short and Incomplete History

Here’s a fun fact: McKay’s is not an original Knoxville business, nor is it a Tennessee company. It began in North Carolina in 1974, as an experimental idea focused on access to books. Its founders, Anne and Phillip Jacobson, envisioned a kind of “free enterprise library” where people could bring in used books, receive store “credit” or cash, and then use that cash or credit to acquire other books. This created a self-sustaining circulation system, long before GameStop flooded the market with this very same model.

The issue, of course, with this “self sustaining system”, is that you have to set it in motion by giving up something that you already possess for less than its value. (I looked, and libraries existed in 1974. But I suppose that’s neither here nor there when free enterprise is available.) The store opened under the name Edward McKay Used Books, a combination of the middle names of its founders, which became the McKay’s after ownership split.

In 1985, McKay’s arrived in Knoxville, where it quickly became the chain’s flagship store. Located off Papermill Drive, it grew into a massive warehouse-style shop. For many Knoxville locals, as well as our plethora of college students that we host, McKay’s became a cultural landmark, rather than just a bookstore.

McKay’s evolved significantly during the late 1980’s through the 2000’s. It added vinyl, tapes and CDs, and expanded into VHS tapes, DVD, Blu-Ray. Inevitably, video games, electronics, musical instruments, and collectibles all ended up at McKay’s. By the 2000s, it functioned more like a hybrid media exchange warehouse than a bookstore. Our capitalist friends would point to this diversification as what helped it survive while many traditional bookstores struggled with chain conglomerates and Amazon.

For decades the Knoxville store was a hangout for students, collectors, and families. It was THE place to discover cheap books, rare media, niche interests, and it remained one of the few remaining large-scale secondhand media stores. It was so successful that McKay’s expanded further: opening a location in Chattanooga in 1990, and then another location in Nashville in 2007, becoming a regional institution across the Southeast. McKay’s had a passionate and devoted following, with many people visiting on a regular basis for decades.

And then, out of seemingly nowhere, McKay’s announced they were closing.

Well, not all the McKay’s. Just the one in Knoxville. To the casual observer, this came as a shock at the loss of a long-standing independent retail anchor in our city.

To more than a casual observer, the closure still came as a shock. Just for a very different reason.

Part 2: Behind the Bookcase

“No business which depends for existence on paying less than living wages to its workers has any right to continue in this country,” said Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Quoting directly from an article by the CWA (Communication Workers of America) published on October 12, 2002:

“There are workers who have been employed at McKay’s anywhere between five years to twenty plus years. We love our jobs and care about our customers who depend on our services,” said Aaron Hege, a Book & Media Buyer at McKay’s with 25 years of service. “But so many of us are struggling with the long hours, lack of adequate compensation, unfair scheduling, and other challenges that are making it difficult for us to continue to do our jobs. At the beginning of the pandemic, some of our long-term employees were fired without an explanation. It made the rest of us feel expendable. We are the reason McKay’s continues to be a successful business and all we are asking is for that to be reciprocated in the way we are treated. The changes we are fighting for will have a lasting impact on improving our working conditions and put us in a position to serve our customers better.”

Workers affiliated with the CWA began collecting union authorization cards in 2022. A majority of employees supported unionization, but, unsurprisingly, management declined voluntary recognition. In turn, workers filed for a National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) election. In November of 2022, employees voted to unionize, forming CWA Local 3805. This made the Knoxville location the first McKay’s store to unionize.

Before the glow of the election could fade, things became contentious between the union and management. The workers entered negotiations for a first contract, focusing on wages and cost-of-living adjustments; scheduling practices; and benefits, such as sick leave. During the beginning of this negotiation, the union accused the company of delaying bargaining and not negotiating in good faith.

As the negotiations dragged on, the union filed multiple unfair labor practice charges with the NLRB. By 2025, federal authorities found merit in several of the allegations, including firing a union bargaining committee member as retaliation, refusing to meet regularly for negotiations, and treating union workers differently from non-union stores, most notably in pay disparities. These complaints escalated the dispute from a local labor issue to a federal labor case.

Union members held demonstrations, including wildcat strikes (wherein workers walk straight off the job in protest) and picketing. This was now no longer just a first contract negotiation, but a street fight. And many were watching. In August of 2025, the NLRB made it known that it will in fact be investigating complaints against the company for the following.

  • 8(a)(1) Coercive Statements (Threats, Promises of Benefits, etc.)
  • 8(a)(5) Repudiation/Modification of Contract [Sec 8(d)/Unilateral Changes]
  • 8(a)(5) Refusal to Bargain/Bad Faith Bargaining (incl’g surface bargaining/direct dealing)
  • 8(a)(3) Discharge (Including Layoff and Refusal to Hire (not salting))

By February of 2026, McKay’s announced it was closing its Knoxville store. As the announcement rippled through the greater Knoxville area, the big question was why. Why would a successful, thriving pillar of the community just close up shop, take its ball, and go home?

The official statement released by the company is that they have sold the land. Not, mind you, that they had sold the land and were relocating to a different space, just that they had sold the land and were closing. The union (CWA) alleges that the closure is in retaliation for unionizing.

Because this matter is far from settled, you can decide for yourself the reason, but if you have read this far, a straighter line could not be drawn by using a ruler. Looking to Roosevelt, McKay’s was successful because the human beings who worked there made it that way, to their own detriment. They deserve to be treated with respect, dignity, and a living wage. If that cannot happen, then the company is not actually a success. It is just another capitalist pariah.

Workers struggle every day in Knoxville for fair wages, fair treatment, and a voice in what they do for a living. It’s past time for you to help them, in whatever capacity that you can. Be it time, money, expertise, or even an unsure, trembling voice that can add to the rising chorus.

Jason does not use AI in the conception, research, or composition of any article, ever.

Sources used: CWA, WVLT8, Tennessee Bar Association

National Perspective

‘Is This a Just War?’: A Christian Socialist Looks at Tehran

Joshua Raposa

While there are recent reports in the press that Donald Trump would like a “speedy end” to the Iran war (kinda like polishing off a dirty glass, turning on “Closing Time,” and watching the bar de-populate), this war may continue into the near future: after all, this was always out of his control, despite appearances.

With that in mind, I think there are a hundred reasons why we shouldn’t go to war with Iran. A few perspectives come into mind across a number of different registers, for example:

  • Invading Kharg Island would be an operational disaster (military)
  • This war continues to isolate the United States in the global order (geopolitical)
  • This war causes higher prices at the pump and grocery store for average U.S. consumer and voter (U.S. working class) and average global consumer and voter (global working class)
  • This war is in breach of the U.N. Charter and the U.S. and Israel’s conduct during this war violates various aspects of the Hague Regulations and St. Petersburg Convention (international law)
  • This war will cause further radicalization of Islam in the Middle East and increase the likelihood of terrorism on U.S. soil (history)
  • It’s not nice to bomb people (common sense)

And so on.

However, I recognize that we live in Tennessee in the South, so many of these “languages,” if I can use that term loosely, may not resonate with you.

That’s why I chose to argue against the Iran war from a perspective that many of us in the area are familiar with: Christianity.

There are a number of schools within the Christian tradition as it relates to the concept of war but the school with the most intellectual weight and strict-ish guidelines is “just war theory”, so let’s try to give this war the benefit of the doubt and see if it is moral or not.

“The principles of the justice of war,” according to the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “are commonly held to be: having just cause, being a last resort, being declared by a proper authority, possessing right intention, having a reasonable chance of success, and the end being proportional to the means used.”

An important point to note is that if any one of these criteria are not met, then the war is not considered just. Let’s look at these criteria, what they mean for Christianity, and what they mean for understanding what’s happening in Iran right now.

’Lookin’ at the Criteria’

  • Just cause: A “just cause,” in this theory, is basically just a “good reason” for doing something. However, “justice,” as a political idea, is always defined within an ethical framework of good/evil. So, what is “just” according to Christianity? Well, this is going to be found in Jesus’ teachings. In Matthew 22:36-39, Jesus teaches: “‘Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind.’ This is the first and greatest commandment. And the second is like it: ‘Love your neighbor as yourself’” (Matthew 22:36-39).

    The clear surface reading of the “second” commandment is straightforward and prescriptive in the same manner as the Ten Commandments: “Love your neighbor as yourself” is an injunction by Christ to treat others as we would treat ourselves. But why? Well, I think this depends on how the second commandment is “like” the second one: in the Gospel, Jesus teaches that his behavior ought to be a source of imitation: “Love one another as I have loved you“ (John 13:34-35). In other words, self-sacrificial love, as symbolized by the events of the Crucifixion, provides the core model for what is considered to be a “good Christian.” We might say, then, that in the context of politics, the extent to which we say someone embodies self-sacrificial love is the extent to which we, as Christians, say that someone is “just.” To translate our previous question of is there a “just cause” in Iran into the Christian context: Did we have a “self-sacrificial loving reason” for bombing another country? Unless, dear reader, you can square the circle of how you can both “love someone” and “bomb them to Hell” simultaneously, I believe the answer to that question is in the question itself.

  • Last resort: This one requires a bit of a rehashing of the newspaper timeline, so bear with me because the “why” is bound up in the context and, in particular, around the question of nuclear weapons. According to the Associated Press “President Donald Trump told reporters at the White House on Friday afternoon that he was not pleased with the way that nuclear talks had been going with Iran” (February 27). Within the theory, “last resort” means “when all else fails,” so if the “nuclear talks” could’ve been handled any other way (barring Mr. Trump’s low or medium-level pleasure levels), then the war would be considered unjustifiable.

    The AP on Feb. 26, a day before Operation Epic Fury started: “Iran also hopes to avert war, but maintains it has the right to enrich uranium and does not want to discuss other issues, like its long-range missile program or support for armed groups like Hamas and Hezbollah.”

    A few things about this passage jump out.

    (1) “Iran […] hopes to avert war”: The Iranians went into the nuclear negotiations in good faith and have done so over the past year and a half. This is a good article containing a timeline of how we got here.

    (2) “Iran […] does not want to discuss other issues, like its long-range missile program or support for armed groups like Hamas and Hezbollah”: If the stated concern was with the nuclear program, then the war could’ve have been avoided if the U.S. removed these other aspects of the negotiations and merely focused on the nuclear program. I’ll direct you to some reasons for why I believe negotiations were designed to fail in this manner.

    (3) “Iran […] maintains it has the right to enrich uranium”: Iran has the right to enrich uranium for commercial purposes under international law. (See Article III, subsection (3) of the Non-Proliferation Treaty). Of course, there’s always the chance that they could turn that into a nuclear weapon, but it’s highly unlikely for several reasons.

    • First, Iran faces some of the most international nuclear oversight. If there were so much as a weaponized radioactive sneeze in Tehran, the entire Western world would obliterate Iran. The Iranians know this. It doesn’t make sense to conclude that the Iranians want a nuclear weapon unless you assume that all Iranian statesmen are raving Islamic lunatics. And if you do, okay, but on what grounds? There are now calls from Iranian hardliners for a nuclear weapon, but it would be silly to not read that as a response to explosions in your main cities and capital.

    • Second, it has been an institutional part of Iranian culture to oppose nuclear weapons on the grounds that the weapons cannot discern between civilians and combatants: given that the killing of an innocent life is impermissible in the Islamic tradition, this would be unjustifiable. This was the religious position of Iran’s Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei in 2003 and remained the same until his assassination by the U.S. and Israel.

    • Third, and perhaps most importantly/convincingly, the U.S. intelligence community, in particular the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), hasn’t found any concrete evidence that Iran was pursuing a nuclear weapon since 2003: “The U.S. intelligence community and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), the U.N. nuclear watchdog, have said that Iran shuttered a nuclear weapons development program in 2003.”

    The Trump administration clearly could’ve leveraged more opportunities for dialogue and honest diplomacy. Therefore, the “last resort” criterion is not met.

  • Proper authority: Proper authority for a war, in the just war tradition, just means that the war is conducted between two states. This condition is fulfilled.

  • Right intention: The IEP defines “right intention” as: “The general thrust of the concept being that a nation waging a just war should be doing so for the cause of justice and not for reasons of self-interest or aggrandizement.” Because in “just cause,” that the “end” is incompatible with the Christian version of justice, this criterion is not met.

  • Reasonable chance of success: See “Kharg Island disaster” article above or look at Iraq post-2003 to get a sense of what “success” looks like in Iran. Not a chance this criterion is met.

  • The means are proportional to the end: If the ends have not been laid out, how can you make the determination to say if it were proportional or not? Without a clearly defined end, a proportionality test cannot ensue. This criterion cannot plausibly be met.

’There’s a bigger picture here’

But ignore that technical point and listen to Jesus, here:

“You have heard that it was said, ‘An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.’ But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil. But if anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also. And if anyone would sue you and take your tunic, let him have your cloak as well. And if anyone forces you to go one mile, go with him two miles. Give to the one who begs from you, and do not refuse the one who would borrow from you (Matthew 5:38-39).

Are we seriously to believe that Man who tells you to “turn the other cheek”; who told Peter in the Garden of Gethsemane, when the Roman solider sought to execute him, to put away his sword because “those who live by the sword die by the sword” (Matthew 26:52); the Man who said nothing to those who beat and humiliated him because it would’ve violated the principle of unconditional self-sacrificial love (Mark 14:61); who forgave Roman soldiers that drove nails into His feet and hands because mercy and forgiveness is logically entailed by unconditional self-sacrificial love (Luke 23:34); who said that we adults do not enter the Kingdom of God unless we “change and become like little children” because children can embody the purest form of love (Luke 18:16), He is going to give his okay to war?

‘Yes,’ Jesus says, ‘It’s okay to ransack and ruin churches and places of worship. It’s okay to destroy the region of the world where I grew up. I’m okay with you killing people as they pray by candlelight (do you think there aren’t any Christians in Iran?). I’m okay with your torture, your murder, your rape, your extrajudicial executions, and hi-tech massacres raining from the sky in cluster munitions and white phosphorus.’

‘Yes,’ Jesus says, ‘Let’s pretend. Here’s a suit, a tie, and a military. Now, imagine you are God and you can run your own Old Testament. Pick your favorite Sodom and Gomorrahs, where you have the right to decide who lives and who dies.’

Can you imagine Him ever saying this?

I refuse to believe that He who “died for the forgiveness of sins” just to teach us a lesson about unconditional love for our neighbors would look at what’s happening in Iran and give his Blessing. But before I, and perhaps we, become too righteous, maybe we should first ask who will judge us if we fail to try to stop this war?

“For in the same way [we] judge others, [we] will be judged, and with the measure [we] use, it will be measured to [us]” (Matthew 7:2).

In the spirit of Christian love and socialist solidarity, DSA calls on our Christian Brothers and Sisters to join in the struggle against all wars in a shared sense for the sacredness of human life and in the name of universal love and say no to the War in Iran.
