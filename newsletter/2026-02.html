The Knoxville Free Press: February 2026

Thursday, January 29, 2026

Welcome back to The Knoxville Free Press, the monthly newsletter of the Knoxville Democratic Socialists of America (DSA).

If you’re interested in joining the work of the Knoxville DSA, please join us at our next monthly meeting (the fourth Monday of each month) or consider joining one of our working groups. Please see the end of the newsletter for other ways to get involved.

Local Perspectives

Unlock Me

Jason Galvas

Chilhowee Park, or as city leaders would like it to be called, “Chilhowee Park and Exposition Center,” sits at the center (pun intended) of profound glaring inequality in our city. If you navigate to Chilhowee Park’s website, the first thing you encounter is 80 ACRES OF BEAUTIFUL OUTDOORS in what looks like 80-point font. The pictures are beautiful and professional. Striking red Japanese Maple trees in the foreground, a lake and fountain in the background, the warm sun reflecting off of it all.

Photo: Brian Stansberry, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0.

Knoxville is blessed with a plethora of parks. When I was learning how to use my new camera, I would spend hours wandering around Adair Park. It was quiet, nature felt very close, even right in the city. It was perfect for a nervous system reset. I encourage everyone to visit a Knoxville park to get off your phone and touch grass.

Unless you live near Chilhowee Park. You can’t do that there, because the gates are locked all the time.

Let me be more precise. The southside, which contains the Midway and all those pretty pictures on the website, is behind a locked gate and not accessible to the public. The northside is open to the public during the day. It’s not a greenspace, nor can it really be called a park. It is a parking lot for The Muse and overflow parking for the Zoo.

The neighborhood surrounding Chilhowee Park, known collectively as Park City, is one of the most economically disadvantaged areas of Knoxville. So, while the rest of the city has easy access to greenspaces, the east side of Knoxville is cut off from easy access to anything, even a park. Luckily, and in a show of the collective power of people, that may change.

If you have been in the Park City neighborhood recently, you cannot help but notice bright yellow signs in many of the yards. The message on the sign is simple: Unlock Chilhowee Park. This movement is spearheaded by the Chilhowee Park Neighborhood Association. I spoke with Cheri and Gary Matthews, members of the association and longtime residents of the neighborhood, about the signs and the community’s next steps.

“There has always been the neighborhood association that meets on a monthly basis,” said Gary Matthews. “We really ramped up, along with many others, when the city tried to sell part of the park to Emerald Youth Foundation.”

Emboldened by the success of shutting down the park sale, the surrounding neighborhoods want to take the next logical step: opening the park to the community’s residents. “It’s a green space that is locked away from the very people that live here,” says Cheri Matthews. “And that’s not right or fair.” Hence, it is time to Unlock Chilhowee Park.

A Facebook page has sprung up alongside the signs: Friends of Chilhowee Park. I encourage all to join, so you are aware and up to date on what is transpiring. The next big step for the community group is a letter that will be distributed among city leaders, as well as being read at the February City Council Meeting.

It is past time for the east side of the city to have the same amenities as the rest of Knoxville. Access to green spaces should not be determined by zip code and economic status. It is as much a human right as healthcare, food, or water. As Lakeshore Park likes to exclaim, ‘It’s Your Park!’ The residents of Park City are loudly demanding ‘It’s Our Park! Unlock It!’ It is encouraging and invigorating to see the community rally around a movement like this. It joins the growing chorus of voices here in Knoxville, not just proclaiming it’s our park, but it is, in fact, our city.

Of Note

Jason Galvas

  • At the January 20th City Council meeting, the Council approved the purchase of 12 new drones for the police department, costing $9.4 million dollars. Mayor India Kincannon appealed to public safety in her pitch for the drones. While drones are not new in other cities, the same concerns about their use will be present here: privacy violations (say, tracking protestors), lack of accountability, due to inconsistent laws, or no laws, and the potential for bias in data collection (especially in minority communities). Councilmember Amelia Parker was the only no vote, citing, correctly, a need for more transparent information about how the drones will be used.

  • Applications for its Community Agency Grants program for the 2026-2027 fiscal year are now being accepted by the City of Knoxville. The program supports nonprofit organizations that align with the city’s goals in areas including public safety, health, and neighborhood connectivity. Applications are accepted in two categories: arts and culture and community and social services. Prospective applicants must operate within the Knoxville city limits, be registered as a charitable organization with the Tennessee Secretary of State or have a current exemption, and have had their 501(c)(3) nonprofit status for at least five years.

  • U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement conducted a raid at a construction site on January 13th. The construction site was directly across the street from Hardin Valley Academy, where our children go to school. The raid, predictably, was chaotic, with people running in every direction, and heavily armed people swarming the entire area. And the school? They had no idea. No one let them know what was going to happen. No safety measures were implemented. This sets a dangerous precedent. How long will it be before this happens when school is letting out and there are students caught in the middle of some other raid, in the way of poorly trained federal agents?

National Perspectives

ICE, DHS, and the Boiling Point of America’s 25-year Police Buildup

Siena Fulton

On January 20, the House of Representatives passed a bill that cleared 10 billion dollars in further funding for ICE ahead of another shutdown. Three days later, workers in Minneapolis took to the streets as part of a general strike with the goal of ending the Trump administration’s brutal crackdown in the city, which has now gone on for most of January and has become markedly more intense after the killing of Renee Good on January 7. ICE has snatched people from their homes and cars, taken children from their schools, beaten protestors, shot another individual in the face and generally disregarded the human rights and liberties that are guaranteed to the residents of the United States. Most recently, another civilian, Alex Pretti, was executed in the street by a gang of ICE agents, sparking even more nationwide outrage. It has been a busy and trying few weeks, with dozens of arrests, brutalities, and injustices, but let’s examine some of the specifics that make this ICE occupation so terrifying for the rest of the country.

Renee Good and Alex Pretti’s killings were in cold blood, and demonstrate a total lack of discipline in the ranks of the untrained ICE blackshirts roaming the streets in Minneapolis, at best. What theirs murders really were, and what we must understand them as, were acts of cruelty and violence aimed at demoralizing the working people and the concerned citizens of our country. While Obersturmführer Greg Bovino prances around the city in his SS-reminiscent overcoat, his boys in ICE must be masked, or their faces blurred, in every photo (and we pay these people’s salaries!). Unmarked vehicles prowl the streets of Minneapolis. Make no mistake: this posturing and obsession with aesthetics and appearances is all part of the Trump administration’s cultural warfare. But the projection of power is not enough. To maintain absolute control, ICE and DHS need to spark fear in the hearts of their own countrymen.

Photo: Chad Davis, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

We must also look past this administration to the conditions that made this unprecedented spike in abuse possible.

Since the 9/11 attacks, the country has been in a consistent National State of Emergency, which has been renewed by every president since George W. Bush and remains in effect today. The Patriot Act, passed originally in October 2001, enables the expanded surveillance of phone calls, banking, and computer usage of American citizens. It was revealed by whistleblower and then-NSA agent Edward Snowden as having been used to capture and store an unprecedented amount of data on regular people. These laws are a defining factor in the consolidation of our modern police state and buttress the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), which was created in 2003 and currently houses ICE. The powers granted to DHS culminate today in declarations like the NPSM-7, which allows individuals and organizations to be targeted by State anti-terrorism task forces for “anti-American”, “anti-capitalism”, and “anti-Christian” beliefs. This ordinance is the offspring of the Patriot Act, which, even though Snowden’s report brought the abuses of the Act to the public view, has remained relatively intact. The continuation of surveillance per the Patriot Act has allowed NPSM-7 to come into full force and further enabled our rampant police state. Militarized cops and ICE agents are completely free to monitor and persecute American citizens based on political concerns.

Besides NPSM-7, a secret ICE internal memo was leaked in which the agency declares that warrantless raids can be made on homes in the case of “probable cause”. This is totally unprecedented. While Federal judges have ruled against this, as it is clearly a violation of the Fourth Amendment, the memo’s existence is a huge step in the direction of all-out violence against “we the people”. It means that ICE will keep trying to achieve total operational independence and the ability to strong arm anyone who they see as a threat to their inhuman mission of mass deportations. No probable cause, no warrants, no rights. Through racist recruitment tactics, where they utilize memes and imagery from Nazi imageboards and forums to recruit unstable, racist young men, promise them a bonus, and set them loose on the streets.

It is here that I want to call a direct parallel to another state, one of our closest allies, in the Middle East: Israel, the settler-colonial paradise that has engaged in a brutal genocide against its indigenous Palestinian population. Using American arms and intelligence as much as our tax dollars, the Israeli “defence” force engages in no-knock raids against Palestinians in the West Bank, forces evictions, and uses brutal crackdown tactics. The IDF trains regularly with American police departments across the country, including in Tennessee.

Assisted by surveillance technology, ICE is becoming more militarized and deploying tactics learned from the IDF. ICE agents have admitted to using a database tracking civilians who film them. The IDF has employed AI and similar surveillance techniques to decide who and what to attack. These tactics are so closely aligned, it is impossible to ignore the similarities. While the IDF operates in an open-air prison masquerading as a warzone, ICE operates on the streets of Minneapolis, still under the jurisdiction of the first amendment. Highly illegal operations like this are why ICE agents stay masked up, anonymous, their faces blurred. Many inside DHS must be aware that what they are doing is illegal, and that someday, somebody will have to answer for it. In response, instead of changing their mission, they are hiding from the long, inevitable arm of justice.

These details are important to the fight against ICE and the siege of Minneapolis today. Understanding the basis of power that has been built against the American people over the past 25 years can change how we approach ICE. While some of the administration’s actions are hollow and aimed purely at propaganda, like the aesthetics employed by their “operators” and social media, much of ICE’s actions are part of a longer, larger buildup of police and military power that started after 9/11 and borrows from the criminal Israeli military.

Today, this power is directed at our immigrant neighbors, who need the help of US citizens now. We must learn from Minneapolis and be prepared to organize in the event of an ICE mobilization in Knoxville. Monitoring ICE patrols, creating mutual aid stations, and sniffing out ICE lodgings have all been integral to the fight in Minneapolis. Our ability to monitor and persecute these agencies is our greatest asset. Applying constant pressure seems to be fairly effective in drawing public attention. However, understanding the root of militarization is equally as important as organizing and mobilizing.

International Perspectives

The Greenland Crisis: A Primer

Joshua Raposa

Are you a bit confused about what’s going on with Greenland?

So were we! But we did a little digging to try to lay out the essential questions and answers. Below is a primer on the Greenland Crisis.

Of course, we skipped some information for reasons of space, but we hope some of these questions/answers can cut through some of the ideological fog that’s out there and can give you a clearer picture of our government’s relationship to the island.

Q: What is Greenland?

Greenland is a semi-autonomous island off the coast of Denmark. A former colony of Denmark, the island was integrated into Denmark via the 1953 Constitution of Denmark.

In 1979, after a home rule referendum, Greenland obtained its semi-autonomous status. The 1979 Home Rule Act provided for

Greenland’s future assumption of administration of its internal affairs in areas including education, labour and social policies, environment, conservation and land use, hydropower resources, fisheries and hunting, healthcare, media, flag, land use, trade, taxation and competition (Greenland: Caught in the Arctic geopolitical contest).

Further responsibilities within the category of “internal affairs” were transferred from the Danish government to Greenland in a 2009 referendum (Act of Greenland Self-Government).

Under this agreement, matters of “internal affairs” remain the prerogative of Greenland’s parliament; however, matters of foreign policy and international security are responsibilities assumed by the Danish government.

Photo: Quintin Soloviev, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

Q: Why is Greenland so important?

There are a number of reasons for Greenland’s renewed geopolitical significance.

The first has to do with mineral resources.

A 2023 report published by the Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland found the island has “25 of the 34 critical minerals identified in the EU’s official critical raw materials list.” As a result, these minerals (among others) have been deemed “critical to US national security and economic stability,” and could be responsible for approximately a quarter of the world’s output in rare earth metals/mineral resources.

The second has to do with its location.

According to the EU, “Greenland is positioned within the Greenland-Iceland-United Kingdom gap, an area of the northern Atlantic Ocean forming a critical passage for vessels moving between the Atlantic Ocean and the Arctic, as well as between North America and Europe“ (our emphasis added).

In effect, controlling Greenland is also about projecting U.S. power both militarily and economically to counter perceived Russian and Chinese influence: “In the event of conflict, the gap would facilitate the rapid deployment of NATO forces across the Atlantic” (ibid).

Q: What is the United States’ relationship with Greenland?

Historically speaking, throughout the Cold War and up until the present, Greenland has served as a site of U.S.-NATO collaboration on matters relating to international security, intelligence sharing, missile defense, and scientific research.

In 1951, the U.S. and Greenland signed a defense agreement, allowing U.S. troops and materiel to be stationed at Pituffik Space Base. The airbase was used throughout the Cold War to conduct reconnaissance operations against the Soviets and to conduct weapons testing.

In the not-so-distant past, the U.S. and Greenland also shared geological survey data and technology to search for mineral resources. A 2019 Memorandum of Understanding between the U.S. and Greenland “provide[d] a framework for Greenlandic and U.S. cooperation on mineral sector governance.”

“Within this framework,” the document noted, “the Greenland Ministry of Mineral Resources and Labour and the U.S. Department of State will jointly fund and operate this summer an aerial hyperspectral survey over approximately 3,000 square kilometers in the Gardar Province, southwest Greenland.”

Q: Where do things stand now?

Presently speaking, however, relations are not so good.

After being allegedly snubbed for failing to acquire a Nobel Prize, President Trump “questioned Denmark’s claim to Greenland, saying, ‘There are no written documents,” and adding, “The World is not secure unless we have Complete and Total Control of Greenland. Thank you!”’ (NYT 01.20.26).

(This is not the first time we’ve attempted to acquire Greenland. See “Greenland is not for sale,” Jacobin, 01.21.25, for a brief history).

Nevertheless, since January 20th, things appear to have calmed down a bit. After pushback from NATO (and, perhaps more notably, after the U.S. stock market took a multi-point plunge), Trump has reeled in his tone. Since then, there have been discussions going on between President Trump and Mark Rutte of NATO to decide a framework for resolving the Greenland Crisis.

“Framework” or no, what has been left out of the Greenland discussion is the most essential component: the input and will of the indigenous population. According to a January 2025 opinion poll, 85% of Greenlanders do not want to become a part of the United States.

As socialists, we stand against imperialism wherever it occurs, especially when it occurs at the behest of our own government. In this case, as in all cases, we must respect the international principle of self-determination and reaffirm the long-forgotten idea that any people of any country have the moral and legal right to determine the course of their own future, free of external influence.
