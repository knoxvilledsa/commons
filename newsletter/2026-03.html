State Perspective

How ICE Operates in East Tennessee

Meghan Conley, Ph.D.

ICE’s reach in Tennessee is extensive, and it has been for years. This might seem surprising since our state, for the most part, hasn’t witnessed the flashbang raids and roving patrols of ICE agents that have drawn headlines and mass outrage in Chicago and Minneapolis. However, the absence of such highly visible acts of force should not be mistaken for an absence of enforcement.

The numbers bear this out. According to data from the Deportation Data Project, an ongoing public records effort that uses FOIA requests to obtain immigration arrest and detainer data from the Department of Homeland Security, Tennessee consistently ranks among the top ten states for total ICE arrests, despite having a relatively small immigrant population.

What accounts for ICE’s efficacy in Tennessee?

Tennessee’s criminal-legal system is thoroughly integrated with ICE. As a result, immigration enforcement in our state is routine, commonplace, and largely invisible.

Knox County is a prime example of how immigration enforcement can be both invisible and highly effective. In 2025, nearly 3,500 people were detained for ICE in the Knox County Jail, a rate of nearly ten people every single day. This represents a dramatic escalation from previous years, including compared to the first Trump administration. We can better understand how enforcement has intensified sharply in East Tennessee by looking at the overlapping contexts of federal enforcement policies, state laws, and local practices.

Chart: Meghan Conley, Ph.D., built from Knox County Jail intake data.

At the federal level, the Trump administration effectively eliminated enforcement priorities in January 2025. Previous administrations directed ICE to focus its limited resources on identifying and detaining specific categories of people, such as those with significant criminal records, threats to national security, and recent border crossers. We can and should interrogate those categories as problematic, but this framework did prioritize far fewer people for enforcement. The Trump administration’s explosion of those priorities means that anyone and everyone who is potentially removable is at risk of detention, regardless of how long they’ve lived in the United States, their ties to the community, or whether they have ever been charged with or convicted of a criminal offense.

State laws that encourage or require collaboration between federal immigration authorities and state and local law enforcement actively support the infrastructure of mass detention and deportation. One example of collaboration is 287(g), which deputizes state and local law enforcement officers with immigration enforcement capabilities. In some jurisdictions, like Chicago, state laws prohibit this kind of collaboration. Tennessee has no such restrictions. As of February 2026, ICE has more than sixty 287(g) agreements with law enforcement agencies in Tennessee. A bill currently moving through the state legislature (HB2219 / SB2223) would require law enforcement agencies to participate in 287(g), rather than merely encouraging them to do so.

Notably, the state’s carceral infrastructure is well-integrated with ICE even without 287(g). Beginning in 2010, Tennessee jails have reported biometric data to ICE, flagging individuals as potentially removable following arrest on a criminal charge. The state’s jails further collaborate with ICE through immigration detainers, which, under some interpretations, permit jails to hold an individual for up to 48 hours beyond when they would normally be released on their criminal charge, without a judicial warrant, until ICE can take custody. (Some courts have held that honoring detainers without a judicial warrant violates the Fourth Amendment, but at present those rulings do not apply to Tennessee.)

Knox County’s voluntary collaboration with ICE goes further still. Through a detention bed contract, the Knox County Jail receives and holds immigrant detainees on civil immigration charges. The pipeline often works like this: someone is arrested and held on a criminal charge in another jurisdiction, their biometric data is transmitted to DHS, and ICE flags them as potentially removable and places a detainer. Under the Trump administration’s expansive enforcement priorities, the severity of the criminal charge is irrelevant. ICE takes custody of the individual upon resolution of the criminal charge, a process made smoother in jurisdictions with 287(g), and the person is transferred to Knox County as an immigration detainee. The result is that Knox County has become a regional hub for immigrant detainees from across East Tennessee and as far away as Middle Tennessee.

Of course, ICE also detains people directly. In Tennessee, like every other state, ICE detains people during routine immigration check-ins, conducts “fugitive operations” (targeted enforcement actions aimed at people with existing removal orders), and carries out worksite enforcement. The latter two are time- and resource-intensive, often requiring extensive surveillance before any action is taken.

However, collaboration with state and local law enforcement is a different kind of tool, one that vastly expands ICE’s efficacy. Through 287(g) partnerships, biometric data sharing, immigration detainers, and detention bed contracts, ICE is able to identify and detain far more people with far less investment of its own time and resources.

The result is a fully elaborated, deeply integrated system of immigration enforcement that doesn’t rely on the visible presence of ICE officers or shocking displays of violence to function. This collaboration makes immigrants who live in our state far more vulnerable to detention and deportation than those who live in places with less cooperative authorities.

Meghan Conley is a cofounder and community organizer with AKIN, a grassroots immigrant rights group in Knoxville. She is the author of Immigrant Rights in the Nuevo South: Enforcement and Resistance at the Borderlands of Illegality (Temple University Press).
